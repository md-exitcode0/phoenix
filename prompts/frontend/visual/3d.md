# 3D: modeling, spatial reasoning, rendering

## Spatial reasoning

- Pick one coordinate convention and say it out loud. Blender: Z up, −Y is
  "front" in front view, units in meters. three.js: Y up, camera looks down −Z.
  glTF: Y up, +Z forward. Most "why is it sideways" bugs are a convention mixup.
- Work in real units. A product 18 cm long is `0.18` m in Blender. Real scale
  makes lights, depth of field, bevels and physics behave believably.
- Before building, write the object as primitives with numbers: "body =
  slightly tapered cylinder, H 0.095, Ø 0.082 top / 0.078 base, wall 0.004;
  handle = C-shaped sweep of a 0.012 × 0.008 oval, 0.06 tall, attached 0.015
  below the rim". This is the blueprint you check the model against.
- Rotations: remember the order (Blender default XYZ Euler). For "aim at
  something", use a constraint or `look_at` instead of hand-computed angles.
  Apply scale/rotation (`Ctrl+A`, or `bpy.ops.object.transform_apply`) before
  modifiers that depend on them (bevel, solidify, array).
- Check from at least 4 views (front, side, top, 3/4). Many models look right
  from one camera and absurd from another.

## Modeling organic forms

- Start from a low-poly cage and a Subdivision Surface modifier (level 2–3),
  not from dense meshes. Control shape with few, well-placed edge loops.
- Cross-sections carry the character: fruit, bottles, limbs and handles are
  rarely round in section (a cucumber is faintly ridged, a pepper is lobed,
  a bottle may be oval). Model the section, then sweep/loft it along a curve.
  Measure the section from a reference photo (edge-to-edge of each face) and
  make it that strong: a 3% ridge modulation renders as a smooth tube.
- Radius profile matters as much as the section: measure where the widest
  point sits, how long the neck is, and how abruptly each end closes. Most
  organic forms taper unevenly; a constant radius with rounded caps reads as
  a sausage.
- Curve + bevel profile, or a spine curve with a Curve modifier, is the fast
  route for anything long and bent. Taper along its length with a taper curve
  or scaled loops.
- Ends and transitions (stems, tips, joints, rims) are where realism lives.
  Give them extra loops and study the reference close-ups.
- Break symmetry at the end: tiny scale/rotation jitter per loop, a slightly
  asymmetric bend, uneven ridge strength.
- For hard-surface objects use bevels on every exposed edge (real edges catch
  light); 0.5–2 mm bevel at product scale, 2–3 segments, harden normals.

## Color management (the #1 cause of pale, washed-out renders)

- Color values typed into shader sockets (`default_value`, ColorRamp stops,
  RGB nodes) are **linear**, not sRGB. A hex swatch divided by 255 is sRGB;
  feeding it in directly makes every color look pale and chalky. Convert each
  channel first: `c <= 0.04045 ? c/12.92 : ((c+0.055)/1.055)**2.4`. (Image
  textures are different: set their color space to sRGB and they convert
  themselves; data maps like roughness/normal use Non-Color.)
- AgX (Blender 4+) and Filmic compress saturated brights; strong yellows,
  oranges and reds drift toward cream. Use the "AgX - Punchy" / "Medium High
  Contrast" look, or Standard view for product shots, and judge color against
  the reference photo side by side, not in isolation.
- Exposure: aim the brightest part of the subject around 0.8–0.9 in the
  render, not clipped. If the whole frame is near white, lower light strength
  or exposure before touching materials.
- Sample the rendered subject's color (e.g. PIL `getpixel` over a patch) and
  compare numerically with the reference swatches. Hue off by more than ~10°
  or saturation off by more than ~20% is visible to anyone.

## Materials (Principled BSDF / MeshStandardMaterial)

- Base color never flat: mix 2–3 tones by a noise or measured gradient
  (e.g. greener toward the ends, browner at blemishes). Keep albedo in the
  sRGB range 30–240. Pure black/white albedo does not exist in nature.
- Roughness is where realism mostly lives. Vary it with a subtle noise
  (±0.05–0.1). Waxy fruit skin ~0.35–0.5, plastic 0.3–0.6, polished metal
  0.05–0.2, fabric 0.7–0.95.
- Bump/normal from procedural noise at the right scale (fine, low strength
  0.02–0.1). Oversized bump is the classic CG look.
- Subsurface scattering for skin, wax, fruit, leaves, soap: low weight,
  radius tinted toward the interior color.
- Spots, bruises and wear: masks driven by noise × curvature/AO, few and
  varied in size, concentrated where handling happens.
- Color zones on natural surfaces fade over a distance, never along a hard
  line: blend with a gradient (ColorRamp over a coordinate or distance) plus
  noise breaking up the edge. A visible seam where two colors meet is an
  instant CG tell.
- For photorealism, texture from real photographs: project or UV-map a
  licensed reference photo (crop the surface, remove lighting gradients,
  tile or mirror it where needed) and drive roughness/bump from its
  grayscale. Procedural textures alone rarely read as real; stretched noise
  along one axis produces visible stripe banding, the classic CG tell.
  Check the texture at 100% for repeats, stripes and seams.
- Cut or broken ends (stems, torn fibers, cut wood) are not flat solid
  discs: give them fibrous texture, darker edges and some relief.

## Lighting and camera

- Studio product look: large soft key at 45° above and to one side, a weaker
  fill opposite (1/3–1/4 of the key), a rim/back light to separate from the
  background, and a ground plane with a contact shadow. Or an HDRI (studio
  softbox HDRI) plus one key area light.
- Use a filmic view transform (AgX with a contrast look in Blender, ACES in
  three.js) for highlight roll-off, and check saturation afterwards (see
  Color management).
- Camera: 50–100 mm for products, low-ish angle for heroism, never perfectly
  dead-on. Slight depth of field (f/4–f/8 at product scale). Frame with
  breathing room.
- Background: seamless gradient or a real surface (wood, linen, stone) scaled
  correctly. The ground shadow sells contact.

## Render settings that matter

- Blender Cycles: 128–512 samples with denoise (OpenImageDenoise) is plenty for
  a still; turn on AO-ish realism via real light bounces, not fake AO. Eevee is
  fine for previews; enable raytracing/shadows/soft shadows if final.
- Render previews small (512 px) while iterating; render finals at 1600–2400 px.
- Save a turntable: 8 frames around Z at 45° steps, montage them into one sheet
  for review in a single look.

## Scripting Blender headless

- `blender -b -P build.py -- args` runs a script without UI. Build the whole
  scene in code (clear defaults, create geometry, materials, lights, camera,
  render settings) so every iteration is reproducible and diffable.
- Save the `.blend` and the renders to the workspace after each pass, with
  numbered names (`pass03_front.png`).
- The user will open the `.blend` in Solid view, which ignores node
  textures and shows each material's Viewport Display color (default grey).
  Set `material.diffuse_color` to the material's average rendered color, and
  save the file with the 3D view in Material Preview, so it looks right the
  moment it opens.
- Useful API: `bpy.data.meshes.new`, `bmesh` for precise vertex work,
  `bpy.ops.mesh.primitive_*` for quick starts, modifiers via
  `obj.modifiers.new(name, type)`, node trees via `mat.node_tree.nodes`.

## three.js / web 3D

- Import GLB for anything authored; procedural geometry for simple, parametric
  shapes. `MeshPhysicalMaterial` for clearcoat, sheen, transmission.
- Lighting: `RoomEnvironment` + PMREM for a cheap studio environment, one
  directional key with shadows, `ACESFilmicToneMapping`, `outputColorSpace =
  SRGBColorSpace`.
- Performance: cap pixel ratio at 2, reuse geometries/materials, dispose on
  unmount, avoid real-time shadows for many objects (bake or use contact shadows).

## Match the reference numerically

Eyes forgive shape errors; numbers do not. For the hero view, render from
roughly the reference's camera angle, then with PIL: threshold subject vs.
background in both images, crop each to its subject, scale to the same
length, and overlay. Report the overlap (intersection over union) and where
the outlines diverge (bend, thickness along the length, end shapes). Below
~0.85 the shape is visibly off, whatever the materials look like. Repeat the
same for a mean color over matching regions. Save the overlay image and
look at it.

- Curved outlines need enough geometry at render size: if you can see
  straight segments along a silhouette, raise the ring/segment count or the
  subdivision level before touching anything else.
- Contact: the object must touch its surface. Check the lowest vertex sits on
  the ground plane and the contact shadow is darkest right under it.

## Review loop for 3D

Render the turntable sheet + a hero shot → compare side by side with the
reference sheet → list differences in silhouette, section, proportions,
color/value, surface, lighting → fix the biggest → render again. Log every
pass. See `visual/looking.md`.
